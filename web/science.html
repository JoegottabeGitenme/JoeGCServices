The Science Behind Trail Conditions

Why we don't just show you the weather model's soil moisture number, and what we do instead.

⚠ Advisory, Not Yet Validated

Nothing on this page describes a shipped, validated product. As of today, FolkWeather's trail pages show raw HRRR/NLDAS weather model output — soil moisture, soil temperature, snow depth — at native 3km resolution, honestly labeled as such. This page documents the physics pipeline we're building to turn that into something better, and exactly how far along it is.

The first validation gate (Rung 1, described below) has now passed against real ground-truth data — but only after we discovered that the published paper's own printed equation doesn't match its own published reference code (see below for the full story). A second, independent check — a different measurement instrument, at the same site, across 59 dates never used to find or fix anything — has since passed too, decisively. That's real, accumulating evidence, not a finished product: one more check, on a completely different research site, is still outstanding before we'd trust this enough to build a live deployment on top of it. Until that's done, no condition or rideability judgment is computed or displayed. This is a deliberate, non-negotiable sequencing choice, not an oversight.

The Problem With Asking a Weather Model

HRRR — the weather model most of this site's data comes from — reports soil moisture on a 3 km grid. That's a real number, but it's an average over 9 square kilometers. A single 3km cell over the Colorado Front Range foothills routinely contains a sunny, south-facing decomposed-granite ridge that's rideable by 9am, and a shaded, north-facing clay drainage 800m away that's still soup three days after a storm. The model reports one number for both.

Worse: land-surface models like HRRR's are tuned so the atmosphere behaves correctly — soil moisture there is a boundary condition for weather forecasting, not a validated ground-truth product. Its absolute level drifts to a model-specific "attractor" that has only a loose relationship to actual volumetric water content in the ground.

Both of these are solved problems in adjacent fields. Foresters use exactly this kind of physics to route logging equipment around soft ground. Precision-agriculture platforms use it to time field operations. Northern-state DOTs use the freeze-thaw half of it to time spring load restrictions on roads. Nobody, as far as we've found, has pointed it at recreational trails — which is the gap this project fills.

The Physics, and Where We Got It

The core method — redistributing a coarse-resolution soil moisture value to fine resolution using terrain — comes from a lineage that starts with TOPMODEL (Beven & Kirkby, 1979): the observation that a landscape's topographic wetness index (how much upslope area drains through a point, relative to how steeply that point drains) predicts where water accumulates, independent of weather.

The specific downscaling equation we implement is adapted from GeoWATCH (Eylander et al., 2023) — a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers system built to give soldiers block-by-block terrain trafficability, extending TOPMODEL with soil transmissivity (how fast water moves through different soil types) on top of pure topography:

θ* = θcoarse + (θs − θwilt)/k · (λ − λ̄)

θcoarse = the coarse (grid-cell or catchment) mean moisture · θs = saturated soil moisture (porosity) · θwilt = wilting point · λ = topographic wetness index at a point · λ̄ = its domain mean · k = 13, an empirical scaling constant

Read it as one physical effect: a point with above-average topographic wetness — a valley bottom, a footslope, anywhere water converges — gets wetter than the grid-cell mean, and by how much depends on how much plant-available water the local soil can actually hold (its saturated moisture minus its wilting point) — sandy soil with a narrow range moves less; clay-rich soil with a wide range moves more. This term needs no weather input to compute; it's a static property of the ground itself, which is exactly why this approach can discriminate between two trailheads that experienced the identical storm.

This equation is not the one we started with, and getting here is worth explaining honestly. The paper's own printed Eq. 1 looks similar but isn't the same: it uses a flat, soil-independent 1/k correction plus a separate term for soil drainage rate (ln Ks). We spent several rounds of validation faithfully implementing and testing that printed equation and consistently found our corrections about three times too large, with the drainage-rate term contributing essentially nothing. The breakthrough came from a lead sitting untouched in the paper's own reference list the whole time: its "Software and Data Availability" section links the original developers' own published code, which they describe as reproducing "the downscaling algorithm." Reading that code directly showed it implements the equation above — soil water-holding range as the correction's scale, no drainage-rate term at all — not the equation printed in the paper's own text. Running that equation, with the same published constant (k=13, never adjusted), is what finally closed the gap (see Rung 1, below). We're not aware of anyone else having noticed or documented this discrepancy; it also resolves a specific sentence in the paper's methodology that had otherwise gone unexplained for months of work ("the calculation of the TWI was modified to use volumetric soil moisture instead of relative soil moisture") — multiplying by a soil's water-holding range is exactly that conversion.

For soil moisture's day-to-day evolution — not just a single downscaled snapshot — GeoWATCH adds a second stage: a flux-difference correction that accounts for vegetation transpiration (following Chen et al., 1996) and direct soil evaporation (following Ek et al., 2003 — the same land-surface-model family embedded inside HRRR itself, so the downscaling logic and the weather model it's correcting speak a consistent physical language), combined with a terrain-aware solar view factor so a north-facing slope and a south-facing slope at the same elevation dry out at different rates. Where the topographic term above answers "given today's average, what does this point look like right now," this second stage answers "how fast does a wet or dry anomaly fade back toward normal," which is what lets a forecast run forward instead of just downscaling a single instant. (An earlier version of this page attributed this stage to the Equilibrium Moisture Theory lineage — Coleman & Niemann, 2013; Ranney et al., 2015 — written before we had the primary source in hand. Having since read it directly: EMT is real, relevant literature, but GeoWATCH's own paper cites it only as related work, not as what its own equations are built from. Corrected here.)

What We Reconstructed vs. What We Verified

We obtained the primary source (Eylander et al., 2023) directly and did a full equation-by-equation audit against it. Two real gaps turned up in what we'd built without it — one substitution the paper's own methodology section explicitly rules out and one equation genuinely absent — both are now fixed. We still track confidence per physics module explicitly, because "we have the paper now" doesn't automatically mean every remaining open question is resolved:

We'd rather publish an honest gap than a confident-looking number we can't stand behind. That posture is deliberate and carries through to the disclaimer on every trail-conditions page: this is an advisory model, not a closure authority.

The Pipeline, Today

HRRR forcing (hourly, 3km) → Sample at trail vertices → Eq. 1 redistribution → Frozen-ground gate → Per-segment aggregate

What exists right now is real, tested code (82 unit tests covering the physics core, the HRRR grid transform, the snow model, and the aggregation logic), wired together end-to-end — but running with a placeholder in place of real terrain data, since the actual 10-meter elevation/soil/canopy stack for Colorado hasn't been built yet. That means today's pipeline run outputs the same value HRRR already gives you at each point, not yet a topographically-downscaled one. The terrain-derived discrimination — the entire point of this project — plugs into an already-built, already-tested slot; it isn't running yet.

We deliberately did not implement Winstral-style wind-driven snow redistribution this round. Front Range trail miles are overwhelmingly below treeline, where canopy shading and slope-aspect radiation dominate melt timing far more than wind-blown drift does — so we built the terrain layers wind redistribution would need (16-direction horizon angles) as a diagnostic, but gated the actual redistribution logic behind evidence: validate the simpler model against real snow-disappearance dates first, see if the errors correlate with wind exposure, and only build the more complex piece if the data says it's worth it.

How We'll Know If It's Right

Before any of this feeds a public rideability claim, it has to pass a validation ladder, in order — each rung gates the next:

Rung 1 — Reproduce a Published Result (passed)

A public research catchment in Australia (Tarrawarra; Western & Grayson, 1998) has thirteen days of dense, ground-truth soil moisture measurements alongside detailed terrain and soil surveys — a clean, no-snow, no-weather-model test of exactly the redistribution equation above. We obtained the real dataset and ran the actual validation. For a long stretch of this project, it didn't pass, and we said so plainly here. It now does — but the path there is worth telling honestly, because it wasn't a matter of trying harder at the same thing; it took finding out the thing itself was slightly wrong.

Four separate rounds of testing — trying coarser terrain resolution, texture-based soil classification instead of measured drainage rate, an alternate terrain-analysis tool's optional smoothing settings, and a whole second physics stage the published model also includes — each produced real, honest, partial progress (cutting the error roughly in half, cumulatively) without ever closing the gap. Every one of those ideas was grounded in something the paper's text actually says; none of them were guesses in the dark, and none of them involved adjusting the paper's published constant to force a match. But something was still structurally off, not just imprecisely calibrated.

The actual answer turned up in a part of the paper we'd cited for months without ever opening: its "Software and Data Availability" section links the original developers' own published reproduction code. Reading it directly showed their real implementation differs from their own paper's printed equation — see "The Physics, and Where We Got It," above, for the equation-level detail. Implementing that version, with the identical published constant (k=13, never touched), passed the gate: our error came in at 0.0332 (measured the same way as the published 0.0321 target — about 3% above it, comfortably inside the 10% tolerance band we'd set as acceptable before ever running this specific test, not adjusted after seeing the result), improving on 9 of the 13 sample dates against a target of 9.

We checked how solid that result is, not just how good the headline number looks: it holds identically across a range of reasonable settings for one input we can't measure directly at this site (vegetation cover) and either way we choose to treat soil variability across the catchment (as a single average, or point-by-point). It does not hold under one specific alternate — but well-established — choice for a different sub-equation (a 1-date miss instead of passing) — a real sensitivity we're disclosing rather than hiding, and a reminder that "passes" here means "passes under the paper's own stated choices," not "passes no matter what."

What this does and doesn't mean: it means the physics, implemented as the original developers actually built it rather than as their paper printed it, genuinely reproduces a published result on real ground-truth data with zero constants tuned to make that happen. It does not yet mean this generalizes — passing against the same 13 measurements the discrepancy was found and fixed against is a much weaker claim than passing against data it's never seen. That's exactly why we didn't stop there.

First generalization check: a different instrument, 59 unseen dates — passed

The same catchment has a second, completely independent measurement record: a neutron probe survey, 59 dates, none of which were used to find or fix anything above. We ran the identical configuration — nothing re-tuned, no dial to turn even if we'd wanted to — against it, having first written down, before running it even once, exactly what would count as a pass (beat the simple-average baseline; improve on at least half the dates). It passed decisively: 45 of the 59 dates improved (well past the bar we'd set), and our overall error actually came in slightly better than the paper's own published figure for this exact comparison. One honest footnote: partway through, we found a single physically impossible measurement in the raw 1997-era data (a negative moisture reading — a real sensor-calibration artifact at an extreme dry spell, not something we introduced) and excluded it the same way we'd excluded a similar bad measurement earlier in this project — by name, with a count, not quietly. Fixing it moved the final number by less than 1%, which is itself reassuring: the result isn't hanging on one data point.

This is real, meaningful evidence — a different instrument and 59 dates the fix has never seen both agree with it. It is still evidence from one catchment, though. The remaining, final check — an entirely separate research site on a different continent, with different terrain, soil, and climate — is still outstanding, and nothing past this gate gets built until it's done.

Rung 2 — Do Rider Reports Separate on Our Features?

Before investing further, the cheap test: pull a winter of Trailforks/MTB Project condition reports, join them to the terrain/soil features above, and check whether aspect, soil texture, and canopy actually explain the reported differences. If they don't, that's important to know before building more.

Rung 3 — Snow-Disappearance Dates

Satellite-derived snow cover (Landsat/Sentinel-2) gives a free, per-pixel check on whether our melt timing is right — and specifically the test that will tell us whether the wind-redistribution physics we deferred (above) is actually worth building.

Rung 4 — Forward Testing

Predict, publish, compare against what actually happens. Track the false-"good" rate above everything else — the error that costs both trails and credibility.
