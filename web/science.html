The Science Behind Trail Conditions

Why we don't just show you the weather model's soil moisture number, and what we do instead.

⚠ Advisory, Not Yet Validated

Nothing on this page describes a shipped, validated product. As of today, FolkWeather's trail pages show raw HRRR/NLDAS weather model output — soil moisture, soil temperature, snow depth — at native 3km resolution, honestly labeled as such. This page documents the physics pipeline we're building to turn that into something better, and exactly how far along it is.

The validation gate for any of this (Rung 1, described below) has not passed yet. We now have the real data and have run the real test — it currently fails, for a specific, understood reason (see below), which we're treating as a stop sign rather than working around. Until it passes, no condition or rideability judgment is computed or displayed. This is a deliberate, non-negotiable sequencing choice, not an oversight.

The Problem With Asking a Weather Model

HRRR — the weather model most of this site's data comes from — reports soil moisture on a 3 km grid. That's a real number, but it's an average over 9 square kilometers. A single 3km cell over the Colorado Front Range foothills routinely contains a sunny, south-facing decomposed-granite ridge that's rideable by 9am, and a shaded, north-facing clay drainage 800m away that's still soup three days after a storm. The model reports one number for both.

Worse: land-surface models like HRRR's are tuned so the atmosphere behaves correctly — soil moisture there is a boundary condition for weather forecasting, not a validated ground-truth product. Its absolute level drifts to a model-specific "attractor" that has only a loose relationship to actual volumetric water content in the ground.

Both of these are solved problems in adjacent fields. Foresters use exactly this kind of physics to route logging equipment around soft ground. Precision-agriculture platforms use it to time field operations. Northern-state DOTs use the freeze-thaw half of it to time spring load restrictions on roads. Nobody, as far as we've found, has pointed it at recreational trails — which is the gap this project fills.

The Physics, and Where We Got It

The core method — redistributing a coarse-resolution soil moisture value to fine resolution using terrain — comes from a lineage that starts with TOPMODEL (Beven & Kirkby, 1979): the observation that a landscape's topographic wetness index (how much upslope area drains through a point, relative to how steeply that point drains) predicts where water accumulates, independent of weather.

The specific downscaling equation we implement is adapted from GeoWATCH (Eylander et al., 2023) — a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers system built to give soldiers block-by-block terrain trafficability, extending TOPMODEL with soil transmissivity (how fast water moves through different soil types) on top of pure topography:

θ* = θws − (1/k)(λ̄ − λ) − (1/k)(ln(Ks) − ln(Ks)̄)

θws = the coarse (grid-cell or catchment) mean moisture · λ = topographic wetness index at a point · λ̄ = its domain mean · Ks = saturated hydraulic conductivity (how fast the local soil drains) · k = 13, an empirical scaling constant

Read the two correction terms as two independent physical effects. A point with above-average topographic wetness — a valley bottom, a footslope, anywhere water converges — gets wetter than the grid-cell mean. A point with above-average soil permeability — sandy, free-draining ground — gets drier, because water moves through it faster. Neither term needs today's weather to compute; they're static properties of the ground itself, which is exactly why this approach can discriminate between two trailheads that experienced the identical storm.

For soil moisture's day-to-day evolution — not just a single downscaled snapshot — GeoWATCH adds a second stage: a flux-difference correction that accounts for vegetation transpiration (following Chen et al., 1996) and direct soil evaporation (following Ek et al., 2003 — the same land-surface-model family embedded inside HRRR itself, so the downscaling logic and the weather model it's correcting speak a consistent physical language), combined with a terrain-aware solar view factor so a north-facing slope and a south-facing slope at the same elevation dry out at different rates. Where the topographic term above answers "given today's average, what does this point look like right now," this second stage answers "how fast does a wet or dry anomaly fade back toward normal," which is what lets a forecast run forward instead of just downscaling a single instant. (An earlier version of this page attributed this stage to the Equilibrium Moisture Theory lineage — Coleman & Niemann, 2013; Ranney et al., 2015 — written before we had the primary source in hand. Having since read it directly: EMT is real, relevant literature, but GeoWATCH's own paper cites it only as related work, not as what its own equations are built from. Corrected here.)

What We Reconstructed vs. What We Verified

We obtained the primary source (Eylander et al., 2023) directly and did a full equation-by-equation audit against it. Two real gaps turned up in what we'd built without it — one substitution the paper's own methodology section explicitly rules out and one equation genuinely absent — both are now fixed. We still track confidence per physics module explicitly, because "we have the paper now" doesn't automatically mean every remaining open question is resolved:

We'd rather publish an honest gap than a confident-looking number we can't stand behind. That posture is deliberate and carries through to the disclaimer on every trail-conditions page: this is an advisory model, not a closure authority.

The Pipeline, Today

HRRR forcing (hourly, 3km) → Sample at trail vertices → Eq. 1 redistribution → Frozen-ground gate → Per-segment aggregate

What exists right now is real, tested code (82 unit tests covering the physics core, the HRRR grid transform, the snow model, and the aggregation logic), wired together end-to-end — but running with a placeholder in place of real terrain data, since the actual 10-meter elevation/soil/canopy stack for Colorado hasn't been built yet. That means today's pipeline run outputs the same value HRRR already gives you at each point, not yet a topographically-downscaled one. The terrain-derived discrimination — the entire point of this project — plugs into an already-built, already-tested slot; it isn't running yet.

We deliberately did not implement Winstral-style wind-driven snow redistribution this round. Front Range trail miles are overwhelmingly below treeline, where canopy shading and slope-aspect radiation dominate melt timing far more than wind-blown drift does — so we built the terrain layers wind redistribution would need (16-direction horizon angles) as a diagnostic, but gated the actual redistribution logic behind evidence: validate the simpler model against real snow-disappearance dates first, see if the errors correlate with wind exposure, and only build the more complex piece if the data says it's worth it.

How We'll Know If It's Right

Before any of this feeds a public rideability claim, it has to pass a validation ladder, in order — each rung gates the next:

Rung 1 — Reproduce a Published Result (failing, but the gap has narrowed a lot)

A public research catchment in Australia (Tarrawarra; Western & Grayson, 1998) has thirteen days of dense, ground-truth soil moisture measurements alongside detailed terrain and soil surveys — a clean, no-snow, no-weather-model test of exactly the redistribution equation above. We obtained the real dataset and ran the actual validation. It still doesn't pass, and we're not going to dress that up — but where we started (every date coming out three times worse than simply guessing the catchment average) and where we are now (roughly on par with that same simple guess, with one date landing within 1% of it) are very different places.

We tested three concrete, specific ideas for what was causing the gap, each grounded in something the source paper actually says, rather than guessing in the dark:

Idea 1 — maybe the published calibration assumes coarser terrain data than the fine-detail survey we're using. The paper mentions a coarser default resolution elsewhere, but re-reading its own description of this specific comparison, it explicitly says the fine-detail survey was used here. We tested it anyway to be sure, and confirmed empirically: going coarser doesn't help — it actively breaks things, because this catchment is too small to represent meaningfully at that scale. Ruled out, cleanly.

Idea 2 — the paper's own description of this comparison lists soil texture as an input, not the directly-measured drainage-rate data we'd been using. We built a standard, published soil classification method and looked up standard reference values for each texture instead. This measurably reduced our error — but digging into why, it turns out this site's soil is nearly uniform in texture (which, again, matches what the paper itself says about this site), so the "improvement" was mostly a case of removing noisy measurement variability rather than adding real explanatory signal. A genuine but partial answer.

Idea 3 — the reference software we adopted last round has some optional smoothing settings, turned off by default, that a production deployment might reasonably enable. Turning them on gave another real, independent improvement.

Combined, these three changes cut our error roughly in half and — more importantly than the raw number — the underlying fit quality genuinely improved rather than just shrinking toward "predict the average everywhere," which is how we can tell this is real progress and not an illusion of one.

We'd also flagged a fourth possibility: that the equation we'd been testing is only half the published model — a second correction stage might exist specifically to pull an over-eager first-stage estimate back toward reality. We built that second stage and tested it directly rather than leaving it as speculation. It turned out to only ever activate on about half the sample dates (a real, paper-specified condition, not a bug), and on those dates it did exactly what we'd hoped — pulled our over-amplified estimate the right direction, every time, never the wrong way — but by a modest amount, not enough by itself to close the remaining gap. One side effect worth mentioning because it's the kind of thing that's easy to get wrong and hard to notice when you have: a technical assumption buried in that second stage (how to convert an evaporation rate into a rate of soil moisture change) turned out, after working through the math properly, to not matter at all for our specific setup — a loose end we'd have otherwise had to guess at, now provably closed instead.

The tempting shortcut here would be to just find a constant that makes the numbers match and move on. We're deliberately not doing that — testing real hypotheses and having some pan out partially, some not at all, is a normal, honest part of debugging, and quietly re-tuning a published constant to pass a validation test would defeat the entire point of having the test. The honest status, after four rounds of testing: real data, real results, real cumulative progress (our error is now less than half what it was when we started), a narrower and better-understood remaining gap, and one specific unexplained sentence in the source paper that remains the leading suspect. Nothing past this gate is being built until it actually passes.

Rung 2 — Do Rider Reports Separate on Our Features?

Before investing further, the cheap test: pull a winter of Trailforks/MTB Project condition reports, join them to the terrain/soil features above, and check whether aspect, soil texture, and canopy actually explain the reported differences. If they don't, that's important to know before building more.

Rung 3 — Snow-Disappearance Dates

Satellite-derived snow cover (Landsat/Sentinel-2) gives a free, per-pixel check on whether our melt timing is right — and specifically the test that will tell us whether the wind-redistribution physics we deferred (above) is actually worth building.

Rung 4 — Forward Testing

Predict, publish, compare against what actually happens. Track the false-"good" rate above everything else — the error that costs both trails and credibility.
